**To reduce form abandonment, first measure which question people leave on, then cut
what they see at the start: fewer fields, one question at a time, no sign-in or
phone number before they've committed. Let them ask what a question means. Keep the
answers of anyone who stops, and remind them with wording that changes each time.**

That's the whole method. The rest of this post is why each step works, where it comes
from, and how it goes wrong.

One warning first. Search this topic and you'll find confident numbers everywhere:
"conversational forms convert 40% better", "inline validation cuts abandonment by
22%". Follow most of them back two or three links and they end at a vendor's
marketing page with no study behind it. (We traced the most popular ones on
[form statistics, traced to their sources](/form-statistics).) I've kept every claim here to a primary
source or to data from a real form, and said so when the evidence is thinner than
I'd like.

## 1. Find the question people leave on

You can't fix abandonment in general. You can fix question seven.

Most form tools report one completion rate for the whole form. That number tells you
that you have a problem, not where. What you want is **drop-off per question**: of the
people who saw this question, how many answered it and carried on? One question
nearly always stands out, and it's rarely the one you'd guess.

Separate two kinds of leaving while you're at it: people who never answered anything,
and people who answered some questions and stopped. They have different causes.

We saw this clearly on a university hackathon registration that ran on chatform this
September. 129 students started a registration and 55 finished, 43%. But roughly three
in four *sessions* ended before a single answer, on a form that asked people to sign in
before question one. (Sessions include reloads and repeat visits, so treat that as a
direction, not a rate.) The biggest loss wasn't in the form at all. It was the door.

**Where this goes wrong:** optimising the wording of question nine when most people
never reached question one.

## 2. Cut what they see before they start

People decide whether to fill in a form by looking at it. A page of twenty fields
announces twenty fields.

Baymard Institute's shopper survey found that
[17% of US online shoppers](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate) have
abandoned an order because the checkout was too long or too complicated. (You'll still
see 22% quoted everywhere; that's Baymard's older survey round.) That's for checkouts, where
people already want the thing. An enquiry form has less goodwill to spend.

Two ways to spend less of it:

- **Delete fields.** For every field, ask what decision its answer changes. If the
  honest answer is "none, but it's nice to have", remove it or ask it later, by email,
  to the people who became customers.
- **Show one question at a time.** The total is the same, but the decision to continue
  is made in small pieces instead of once, at the top of the page. This is the core
  idea behind [conversational forms](/conversational-forms), and the reason even the
  simplest one-question-per-screen builders help.

**Where this goes wrong:** hiding length rather than reducing it. A one-at-a-time form
with forty questions still has forty questions, and people notice around question
twelve. Keep a progress indicator so nobody feels tricked.

## 3. Move the expensive questions later

Some questions cost the respondent more than others: a phone number, a budget, a
sign-in, an upload. Asked first, they're a toll at the entrance. Asked after a few easy
questions, they land on someone who has already invested something.

There's decent evidence that a task which feels already begun gets finished more
often. In a well-known field experiment, Nunes and Drèze gave car-wash customers a
loyalty card needing eight stamps, or one needing ten with two already stamped. The
work left was identical. The second group was about
[twice as likely to finish](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480). A form that opens with an
easy question someone actually wants to answer ("what are you looking for help with?")
borrows the same effect.

If a gate genuinely has to come first, as sign-in did on that hackathon form (it stopped
duplicate registrations), say why on the screen before it. "Sign in so each team can
register only once" is a reason. A bare Google button is a toll.

**Where this goes wrong:** asking for contact details last and losing the people who
abandon, whom you then can't contact at all. The compromise is to ask for an email
early, *with* a reason, and everything costly after it.

## 4. Let people ask what a question means

A form can't explain itself. When a question is ambiguous, the respondent either
guesses, which gives you a bad answer, or leaves.

Survey researchers have tested this for decades. Schober and Conrad found that letting
an interviewer
[clarify what a question means](https://doi.org/10.1086/297818) sharply reduced errors
in the answers, and the effect held in a later
[household telephone survey](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/64.1.1), at the cost of
longer interviews.

On a web form, the cheap version is help text under the question. The better version
is letting people ask. In chatform (on Pro), the respondent can type a question mid-form ("do I
need to bring my own laptop?"), get an answer from notes you've written, and carry on
from the same question.

**Where this goes wrong:** help text nobody reads because every field has it. Put it
only on the questions people actually get wrong. Your answers will show you which.

## 5. Don't punish a short answer

Required fields with validation errors are the most common way a form tells somebody
they've failed. Mostly it gets you a worse answer, typed only to make the error go
away.

The research on open-ended answers points the other way: ask again, don't reject. In a
[600-person experiment](https://doi.org/10.1145/3381804), Xiao and colleagues found that a
chatbot which probed thin answers got responses that were significantly more
informative and specific than the same questions as a web survey. Kim, Lee and Gweon
found people answering by
[chat satisficed less](https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300316), meaning they were less
likely to pick whatever answer ended the question fastest.

In practice: if someone types "n/a" into "what's your budget?", a follow-up ("a rough
range is fine, even under $1,000 or over") gets you a usable answer where an error
message gets you "1".

**Where this goes wrong:** following up on everything. A clear choice answer needs no
follow-up, and a form that interrogates every reply is slower than the one it replaced.

## 6. Keep what they gave you, and go back for them

Somebody who answered six questions and left is the warmest lead your form produced.
Most tools throw that away, or keep the partial answers but never contact the person.

Two things matter if you do follow up.

**Vary the reminders.** Sauermann and Roach ran a web-survey experiment with over
24,000 people and found that reminders
[which changed their wording](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2012.05.003) raised the
odds of a response by over 30% against reminders that repeated themselves. Three copies
of the same email is the weakest version of this. chatform's reminders are written
differently each time and link straight back to the question the person stopped on.

**Measure it honestly.** On the hackathon form, 98 students who left partway got at
least one reminder, and 24 finished afterwards. It's tempting to call that 24
recoveries. It isn't, quite: the form had no holdout group, and some of those students
were always going to come back before the deadline. The only way to know what reminders
earned is to hold a share of people back and send them nothing, then compare. Any
recovery rate quoted without that comparison, including every one you'll see in
vendor marketing, is counting people who might have returned anyway.

**Where this goes wrong:** reminding people who already finished. Check the response
status at the moment each reminder is due, not when it was scheduled.

## 7. Know when a plain form is the answer

Everything above assumes your form is long enough, or ambiguous enough, for abandonment
to be a real problem. A newsletter signup isn't. A three-field contact form with
obvious fields isn't. For those, a plain form on one page is faster, and a conversation
just adds clicks.

A rule of thumb that has held up for us: if any question could reasonably be answered
"it depends", or you've ever had to email someone back to ask what they meant, the form
is a candidate for the fixes above. If not, shorten it and leave it alone.

## Where to start tomorrow

1. Turn on per-question drop-off and find the worst question.
2. Delete or defer every field whose answer doesn't change a decision.
3. Move sign-in, phone and budget questions after the first easy one.
4. Make sure partial answers are saved, and set up reminders that change their wording.

up, and the [research behind conversational forms](/why-conversation-works) has every
